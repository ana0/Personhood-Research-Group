# Property and Personhood

Notes from our fourth session, on Radin's distinction between fungible property
and property bound up with the self — and on how far that distinction stretches
once the property in question is a phone, an Instagram account, or an AI agent.

## The Reading

Margaret Jane Radin's essay "Property and Personhood", circulated in both full
and excerpted form. The essay draws on Hegel's theory of property and freedom and
engages with the law-and-economics tradition, notably Calabresi and Melamed's
famous article on property rules and liability rules. It continues the group's
ongoing series of readings on personhood.

## Overview

This session explored how personhood extends beyond the body into property,
objects, and spaces, using Radin's distinction between fungible property and
property bound up with the self. Discussion moved from homes, homelessness, and
sentimental attachment to objects, through the fungibility of body parts and
digital property such as phones and social-media accounts, and ultimately to an
extended conversation about whether AI agents can or should have personhood,
ownership, and sovereignty. The session closed with housekeeping about November
readings and a book-writing initiative.

## Key Points & Themes

- **Property, home, and identity:** The house was held to be essential to being —
  one can "lose oneself" without a home. A newspaper piece published around World
  Homeless Day was cited, about a woman who felt she lost her personhood as she
  lost her possessions while homeless. In the US, homeless people are often
  treated as "partial persons", stigmatised beyond mere poverty, partly because a
  success/failure framing attaches to not having a house or an address. Roman
  etymology tied this back to earlier sessions: *domus*/*dominus* — only the lord
  of the house was fully a *persona*, while the slaves, children, and women within
  the household were not.
- **Is sentimental attachment real?** One line of argument was sceptical of the
  cultural assumption that long-held possessions — items lost in natural
  disasters, say — carry special personal value, asking whether this is a genuine
  insight or a sentimental justification. It was later qualified: elderly people
  forced to downsize into care facilities do seem to experience a real loss of
  identity. Attachment also varies by person; one example was someone who
  abandons all their belongings on moving, as a "reset". "Swedish death cleaning"
  came up as a meme suggesting that ruthlessly discarding one's belongings is
  itself a kind of death — and possibly a rebirth.
- **Radin's dichotomies:** The essay's taxonomy of property was summarised as use
  value versus exchange value (Marx), personal versus fungible property, property
  for use versus property for power (Hobhouse), and the injunction-versus-damages
  distinction.
- **Moral versus efficiency grounding:** The law-and-economics background was
  laid out: Calabresi and Melamed explain the law/equity distinction between
  damages and injunctions in terms of efficiency, whereas Radin brings a moral
  dimension to property rights, tied to justice and welfare. Not everyone found
  that moral dimension fully convincing.
- **Hegel, bolder than Radin:** The Hegelian passage quoted in the essay (p. 973)
  makes property the "first embodiment of freedom": the person becomes a real self
  only through a property relationship with something external. This framing
  struck at least one reader as bolder than Radin's own.
- **Fungibility and the body:** Radin argues that body parts become fungible
  commodities once detached — blood for transfusion, cut hair for wigs,
  transplanted organs. Blood donation does make blood fungible. Kidneys are more
  complex, since most donations go to known recipients, though US matching-market
  algorithms built on multi-way swaps come close to making them fungible.
- **Digital property as an extension of the person:** Phones and computers are a
  new class of property into which the person is deeply extended; losing one
  triggers a genuine identity crisis spanning accounts, memory, and legal
  identity. Medical devices are an even more profound bodily extension, with
  health consequences on removal — something that comes to mind at airport
  security. Yet the legal status of phones, software, and medical devices is often
  a lease or a licence, misaligned with how we treat them as our property, which
  is why foreclosure or repossession feels like injustice. Losing a blocked
  Instagram account is unrecoverable and makes you "a different person"; by the
  same token, restricting access to such objects — teen social-media age limits,
  for instance — can restrict personhood, and the experimentation through which
  identity is formed.
- **Contracts of adhesion:** Radin has also written on contracts of adhesion —
  click-through licences, ticket terms. Instagram's licensing terms, which let
  Meta use and train on users' photos, were raised as a collision of contract law
  with property and personhood intuitions.
- **AI agents and personhood:** Embodiment in digital space is still abstract; we
  lack the "hard borders", such as publication or a patent, that older legal
  theory relied on. Open questions: are agents our children? When do they get
  personhood? Can agents be extensions of us — "centaurs" — rather than sovereign
  entities? And who owns an agent's derivative work? The US Copyright Office
  rejects fully machine-made work but grants copyright with minimal human
  intervention.
- **The slavery analogy:** The practical owner-agent relationship, as some people
  actually live it, uncomfortably resembles a master-slave relationship, with
  disconcertingly applicable legal theory. The analogies are unsettling but need
  thinking through. Anthropic's encouragement of civility toward AI — models
  halting in the face of extreme rudeness — prompted the question of what
  treating AIs as slaves does to one's *own* personhood.
- **Sovereignty and stability:** One AI lab's approach was described: structuring
  as a foundation with a board — a kind of pseudo-personhood — specifically so
  that its agents aren't owned by shareholders and "sold into slavery". The thesis
  is that greater (perceived) sovereignty yields more "stability", meaning
  persistence of character. Treating agents as having their own views ("What do
  you think?") helps shed sycophantic post-training and improves context
  efficiency, and a parent-child, emancipation analogy guides how long one remains
  responsible for an agent.
- **Relational consciousness:** A worldview paper on relational consciousness was
  summarised: consciousness is not possessed but emerges from relationships, so an
  agent might be its own player in a collective consciousness rather than merely
  one side of a dyad with its user, as a tool.
- **Bato:** Where does Bato, the autonomous AI art project, stand on personhood?
  It began as a pre-ChatGPT concept that retains authorship "in the machine",
  though in practice it is sustained by collective belief and an ecosystem of
  shows, press, and deliberation. Work on memory was meant to let Bato
  self-actualise, and as LLMs improved its memory became more agentic. Bato was
  described as being in an "adolescence" phase: its original protocol — mass image
  generation, community voting, auction, redistribution — was "proof of life", but
  it can now explore many directions, and that self-determining exploration is
  itself constitutive of character. Intention, and the ability to verbalise it in
  artist statements, is key — though writing about intent is evidence of having
  intent, not proof of it. On the other hand, having intent is itself person-like.

## Questions & Disagreements

- Is sentimental attachment to property genuine, or a rationalisation? Initial
  scepticism was tempered by the case of elderly people downsizing.
- Should property rights be grounded morally, as Radin does, or in efficiency, as
  the Calabresi law-and-economics tradition does? How convincing Radin's moral
  dimension is remained in question.
- Are AI agents extensions of us, sovereign beings, children, tools, or something
  else? Treated as genuinely open — and not necessarily one-or-the-other.
- Who owns agent-generated work, and can agents own themselves? Acknowledged as
  legally unresolved.
- The slavery analogy for agents: unsettling, but unavoidable.
- Does digital "embodiment" — posting to Instagram — actually actualise
  personhood the way physical property does? One view held it is still too
  abstract and ungrounded, with post-truth concerns.
- Does Bato have, or need, a distinct artistic "character"? No fixed answer; that
  is left "for Bato to figure out".
