# Making Up People

Notes from our fifth session, on Hacking's claim that new ways of classifying
people bring new kinds of people into being — and on what that means when we are
deliberately constructing personas for AI models.

## The Reading

Ian Hacking's "Making Up People", in two versions of the same title: the original
essay (1986) and a later version published in the *London Review of Books*
(2006). The newer version uses more contemporary examples and presents a more
schematic, numbered framework; the older is more philosophically detailed. Some
participants read only the LRB version.

## Overview

The session explored Hacking's idea that classifications and categories "make
up" new kinds of people through a looping feedback effect between labels and the
people they describe. Discussion moved through examples — multiple personality
disorder, schizophrenia, sex and gender on bureaucratic forms, occupation — and
the roles of science, bureaucracy, politics, and capitalism in constructing
personhood. The conversation then turned to how these dynamics apply to AI, and
the deliberate construction of model "personas", and closed with housekeeping and
a proposal for a public-facing output from the group.

## Key Points & Themes

- **Macro and micro:** Hacking concerns the social construction of groups or
  types of people — a "macro" version of what Erving Goffman describes at the
  micro level, in the management of stigmatised personal identities. The two pair
  nicely.
- **Categories, or personhood?** The title is catchy, but one reading found the
  LRB piece to be more about how science and social science define key
  characteristics of people than about personhood itself — you are still a person
  either way — and struggled to see how socially shaped diagnoses amount to
  "manufacturing" people. Others agreed the text is about categories and types of
  people rather than personhood as such, while wondering whether the long
  historical construction of personhood itself followed patterns similar to the
  ones Hacking describes.
- **Dynamic nominalism and play:** Hacking's playful "dynamic nominalism" and the
  looping effect were emphasised: people voluntarily "try on" new names and
  self-definitions — imagining parts of oneself in conflict, for instance — which
  expands the "play field" of selfhood. Hacking quotes Nietzsche to the effect
  that creating new names can create new things.
- **Persona and role:** This connected to earlier discussions of persona and
  personage. Identifying as an "artist", for example, draws on a culturally
  available role that did not exist in the same form centuries ago, when the
  nearer equivalent was a portrait painter. Such externally named roles shape
  one's sense of self.
- **Categorisation creating new people:** The interesting core is categorisation
  literally creating new types of people, not just roles. Examples: a family
  dealing with schizophrenia through religion rather than medicine when the
  diagnostic category was unavailable to them (from Rachel Aviv's *Strangers to
  Ourselves*); Macaulay's 1835 memo making English the administrative language of
  India, shaping lives generations later; and the bureaucratic shift from "sex" to
  "gender" on forms. Bureaucracies create new people, often unintentionally, and
  the effects can take generations to recede into invisible background.
- **The political and commercial side:** Simone de Beauvoir's "one is not born,
  but becomes, a woman" highlighted the political and dangerous side of labelling
  — the dehumanisation of Tutsis as "cockroaches" in the Rwandan genocide — as well
  as its commercial dimension: pink and blue for babies, labelling in order to
  sell.
- **Science as cover:** Hacking points out that these characterisations are often
  posed as science but are quasi- or pseudo-science masking bureaucratic,
  political, or capitalist activity. Note the short path from a psychiatric
  diagnosis to Oprah, a bestseller, a movie; diet and nutrition fads work
  similarly.
- **Seeing like a state:** James Scott's *Seeing Like a State* was brought in:
  bureaucracy relies on hierarchically defined identities. Academia's rigid
  categories make an unusual title like "scholar in residence" baffling to place,
  and occupation ("occ") scores in US census data are reductive attempts to make
  people commensurable and countable.
- **Constructing AI personas:** The LLM "persona" — the helpful assistant — is
  deliberately constructed in post-training and RLHF. The base model is a "raw
  beast of language" with no consistent persona; the assistant is one of many
  possible characters, which is safety-relevant. We construct model personas
  "more literally than Hacking talks about it for humans."
  - We both construct personas and identify parameters that produce undesirable
    ones — an article was mentioned on a single weight that can make an
    open-weight model "evil".
  - A paper claimed major models share traits with their companies' founders,
    raising the question of whether AI can have unarticulated desires.
  - AI mirrors an "ideal" or "average" personhood, and the exchange is mutual:
    chatbots have prompted a visceral question of whether we are "just weights
    and metrics". We are becoming a human–machine hybrid.
  - AIs will not self-define in isolation; they reflect back what we bring. There
    is a strong pragmatic pull to fit AI agents into existing personhood
    categories, as happened with corporate personhood, whether or not that is
    "innately right".

## Questions & Disagreements

- Is "making up people" really about personhood, or only about categories and
  characteristics? One view pressed that a person remains a person regardless of
  label; others treated categories and personhood as related but distinct.
- Danger versus play: some emphasised the coercive and dangerous side of
  labelling — bureaucratic, political, dehumanising — while others stressed the
  voluntary, experimental, playful dimension of trying on new self-definitions.
  These were treated as complementary rather than resolved.
- Is personhood itself just another constructed classification — a useful
  societal category determining rights and interactions, with subclassifications
  branching outward? One answer: personhood is a constructed concept that did not
  always exist in its current shape, though people were "always people in some
  sense".
- Can we even see personhood from outside our own context, given that the
  observer influences the observed? The idea of an "innate interior person" was
  tentatively traced to Descartes and the Enlightenment.
- What new types of people will the present moment create? Examples raised —
  CRISPR babies, thinking robots, fruit-fly-style connectome scans of humans — but
  the question was left open.
- What self-correcting or stabilising mechanisms act on categories over time —
  autism becoming a spectrum, "we're all neurodivergent" — rather than only the
  amplification of the looping effect? Raised but not fully developed.
