# A Category of the Human Mind

Notes from our third session, on Mauss's genealogy of the person — masquerade to
mask to role to the modern self — and on how much of a person is left once the
social layers are subtracted.

## The Reading

Marcel Mauss's 1938 Huxley Memorial Lecture, "A Category of the Human Mind: The
Notion of Person, the Notion of Self", circulated in both full and excerpted
form. A text of French sociological anthropology, it traces the historical
evolution of personhood through *personage*, *persona*, *person*, and *moi* (the
"me" or self). The group treated it as a dated but productive piece of time
travel.

## Overview

This session worked through Mauss's genealogy of personhood. Discussion centred
on whether personhood is intrinsic or socially and culturally constructed, on
scepticism toward the essay's progress narrative culminating in Kant and German
philosophy, and on the recurring theme of masks and role-playing. Several threads
carried the text forward into contemporary questions of AI personhood, privacy,
cryptography, and online identity. The session closed with logistics for
selecting the next reading.

## Key Points & Themes

- **Socially determined, or intrinsic?** The clearest takeaway for one reader was
  that personhood in the text is not intrinsic but socially determined — a
  function of one's role, one's ancestors, and other external factors. That
  raises the question of what, if anything, is *left* of a person once the
  cultural and historical accretions are subtracted. For ancient cultures — a
  Roman stripped of citizenship and freedom — there may be very little left, a
  frame that is hard to inhabit from a modern, Enlightenment-inflected assumption
  of a core intrinsic self. A structural counter held that humans are
  fundamentally individuated at a basic physical level, independent of any social
  recognition; the real difficulty is the *technique* for socially constructing
  individuation across billions of people — names, legal identity, social
  security numbers, hierarchy.
- **Person as role, descending from the mask:** The main idea was read as
  person-as-role-playing, descended from masking, with the first paragraph of the
  conclusion compressing the whole argument from masquerade to mask to modern
  personhood. Mauss's sequence of terms was mapped out: *personage*, a rule-based
  and relational mask; *persona*; *person*, the moral and responsible subject; and
  *moi*, the interior self that Mauss claims is present in every culture and era.
  The person/persona distinction is genuinely tangled in the text itself.
- **Masks are not historical:** Masks as identity and expression are far more
  ubiquitous across continents, cultures, and eras than the essay's limited
  ethnography suggests — Venetian carnival masks and a Kenyan mask owned by a
  participant were offered against it. They are also alive and well today in
  avatars, usernames, and pseudonyms, despite the text framing them as belonging
  to the past; the image of masks within masks was singled out as the striking
  one.
- **Scepticism about the progress narrative:** The essay was criticised for
  writing from a supposedly impartial vantage point observing the past, rather
  than acknowledging its own situated and contingent position — a possible
  systematic error in positing a detached observer, a "god's view" or Cartesian
  detachment, while describing situated subjects. Crediting Kant and German
  philosophy with bringing "closure" to personhood was called a bridge too far
  within what is a narrow, if important, philosophical tradition. The suspicion
  was that philosophy and social change coevolved endogenously, with social
  change more likely leading philosophy than following it, and the modern
  recognition of universal human dignity is a recent and imperfectly realised
  turn.
- **Public self, secret self:** Is "the self" the public self plus a secret self,
  or is one of the two the real person? We tend today to treat the secret self as
  the real one, but that may not always have been the case.
- **Ritual, extraction, and keys:** One thread took ritual as constitutive of the
  person — performance shapes the person and the person shapes the performance —
  and, drawing on a cypherpunk reading workshop, framed the modern self as
  something extracted and tracked on platforms. Privacy is not secrecy but the
  power to reveal oneself selectively; over-disclosure could destroy the person,
  and the exposure of private thought carries a specific political danger. The
  analogy offered: the private key is the soul of the digital person, the public
  key is the mask.
- **Personhood as relational, and the AI question:** Consciousness
  (compositional) and personhood (political — who gets what) had been held
  separate, but the text prompted a reading of personhood as an extension of a
  relational view of consciousness: our self-understanding is entangled with how
  others see us and how we think they see us. That bears directly on social and
  parasocial relationships with machines, which may require extending some form
  of personhood in order to delineate the lines better. Against this sits a
  tension of control — we can change masks, but law and the immutable experience
  of being constrain how much control we actually have.
- **A meta-project for the group:** Separate out the different aspects of
  personhood and self that the text runs together — the internal self, the moral
  person, the rational person, and the legal person, which may lack subjectivity
  altogether, as a corporation does — and find the boundaries of Mauss's
  framework. The private/public self maps onto the private/public key analogy.
- **Translation:** One participant, confused by the terms, downloaded the French
  original: in French, "moral personhood" overlaps both the moral subject and the
  legal entity, a distinction that fades away in that language. Coming from a
  French-Canadian, Québécois, secular society with deep Catholic roots, this
  landed personally and prompted a re-examination of their own understanding of
  personhood. This genre and era of academic writing is also famously badly
  translated, with meaning lost in the English.
- **From the opening chat:** The recent symposium showed wide divergence in
  participants' views on AI and protocols, from strong scepticism through to a
  view of AI systems as beings we are creating — but the debates were respectful.

## Questions & Disagreements

- Is there an intrinsic person left once the cultural, historical, and social
  layers are subtracted? Unresolved; the physical-individuation argument offers
  one answer.
- Did philosophy drive the social change toward individual dignity, or follow it?
  The session leaned toward social change leading, while granting that Mauss
  himself would credit the philosophers — and that it is most likely a
  coevolving, endogenous mess.
- Is the real self the public self, the secret self, both, or neither? One answer
  was that the self is fluid rather than constant.
- What does it mean to locate subjectivity outside of the self? Asked as a
  genuine rather than a critical question, and left partly unresolved amid
  overlapping audio.
- The distinction between *persona* and *person* confused nearly everyone, which
  appears to be a property of the text rather than of the readers.
- Nobody was certain what the essay is *for* — whether Mauss is arguing a stance
  or simply observing how personhood developed.

## References Mentioned

- Marcel Mauss, "A Category of the Human Mind" — the reading under discussion.
- David Graeber, *Debt* — the discussion of the gift, and of slavery as a kind of
  death, a denial of personhood.
- Erving Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, recommended as the
  modern follow-on to Mauss; also his work on asylums, stigma, and the
  "management of spoiled identity".
- Immanuel Kant — rationality as fundamental to personhood, discussed
  sceptically.
- Jessica Riskin, *The Restless Clock* — history of science; Cartesian detachment
  against a Leibnizian perspective, and the agency and autonomy of living things.
- *The Bourgeois Virtues* trilogy — on the Western, Eurocentric evolution of
  respect for individual dignity. The author was not named in the session;
  commonly Deirdre McCloskey.
- Margaret Jane Radin, "Property and Personhood" — raised again as a candidate
  reading, and already on the [prospective
  readings](../prospectivereadings.md) list.
- Public-key cryptography as a metaphor for soul and mask, by way of a cypherpunk
  reading workshop.
- Venetian carnival masks, and a Kenyan mask owned by a participant.
- Venkatesh — cited for the view, aired at the recent symposium, of AI systems as
  beings we are creating.
