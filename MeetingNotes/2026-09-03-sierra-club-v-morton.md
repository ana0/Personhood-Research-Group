# Sierra Club v. Morton

Notes from our first session, on Justice Douglas's dissent and the idea that a
valley, or a river, might have standing to sue in its own name.

## The Reading

The session centered on the U.S. Supreme Court dissent in *Sierra Club v. Morton*
(also referred to as "Mineral King v. Morton"), particularly Justice Douglas's
dissent on standing. This served as a short "teaser" text connected to the longer
essay chosen for next session: Christopher Stone's "Should Trees Have Standing?",
which the dissent cites.

## Overview

This was largely an organizational kickoff meeting for a new personhood research
group, combining participant introductions, collection of research questions, and
nomination of readings. The group briefly read and reacted to the *Sierra Club v.
Morton* dissent, touching on legal standing, personhood of natural environments,
and the tension between adversarial legal systems and ecological wholeness. The
deep-dive discussion of the longer text is scheduled for the next session.

## Key Points & Themes

- **Introductions and interests:** How fiction writers began basing characters on
  what they saw on TV; property and personhood, citing Margaret Radin's work; and
  the "boundary conditions" of identity — how identity often starts as a porous
  boundary and later gets rendered crystal clear.
- **Group logistics:** A research group page has been set up with a list of
  research questions, so far all written by one person; participants were invited
  to contribute their own questions as threaded posts and to nominate readings. A
  recap bot was unavailable for this call (a limited number of concurrent
  instances), so participants read and typed rather than relying on it.
- **Personhood of natural environments:** The real-world example of a river in New
  Zealand granted legal personhood was raised, along with the Te Awa Tupua Act as a
  case worth examining.
- **Category errors and the function of law:** One line of argument held that law's
  core function is to standardize and discretize in order to scale to an entire
  society, which forces living ecosystems — a whole valley, a river, or its insects
  — to be broken into discrete, separable units. This raised the question of whether
  an ecosystem maps better onto an organization or incorporation than onto a single
  legal entity, and suggested "category errors" in the legal system's framework.
- **Standing as a gating doctrine:** Re-read in light of advancing AI and a
  worsening climate crisis, the dissent is striking on standing as the doctrine that
  gates who can even bring a claim. In an adversarial civil system, aimed at
  equitable relief or damages, this sits in conflict with the balancing role of
  federal agencies: if an organization like the Sierra Club lacks standing, then no
  one can bring an adversarial claim against private interests that would exploit
  natural resources.
- **Compromised agencies:** The text notes the compromised nature of federal
  agencies asked to balance impossibly broad concerns. Justice Douglas is charitable
  here — explicitly saying the agencies aren't corrupt, only asked to balance many
  things — which differs from someone who would "speak for the trees." The critique
  feels even more potent now, with reference to the current Trump administration.
- **Personhood as political tool:** It may be interesting to think of "deep
  personhood as a political weapon" within an adversarial legal system that requires
  an adversary able to make a claim.

## Questions & Disagreements

- Who should be able to be the plaintiff — a human who can show harm, or the
  valley, river, or ecosystem itself? Raised as a central question of the dissent.
- Where do you draw the boundary of what counts as "the valley," river, forest, or
  ecosystem that is the legal subject? Ecosystems have many possible boundaries of
  differing fidelity depending on which aspects you examine. This boundary problem
  does not appear to be addressed in the longer reading.
- How do you standardize and discretize living, interconnected ecosystems into legal
  units without committing category errors? No resolution reached — framed as an
  open provocation.
- How does an adversarial system reconcile with the balancing mandate of federal
  agencies, and who speaks for non-human entities if organizations lack standing?
- No sharp disagreements; the conversation was exploratory and mostly additive.
