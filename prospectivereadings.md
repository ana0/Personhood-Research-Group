## Law and legal scholarship

- **Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017** (New Zealand) —
  the act granting legal personhood to the Whanganui River. Proposed as the focus of
  a full session.
  <https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2017/7/en/latest/>
- **Miriama Cribb, Elizabeth Macpherson & Axel Borchgrevink, "Beyond legal personhood
  for the Whanganui River: collaboration and pluralism in implementing the Te Awa Tupua
  Act" (2024)**, *The International Journal of Human Rights* — a companion to the Act.
  It moves attention away from legal personality and onto three elements of how the
  settlement works in practice: the centrality of Māori kawa (value-based Indigenous
  law), the new institutions and practices the Act established, and the devolution of
  authority to hapū and communities. Its case studies are Te Pūwaha, the revitalisation
  of the Port of Whanganui, and Te Kōpuka nā Te Awa Tupua, the collaborative group
  developing a strategy for the river.
  <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13642987.2024.2314532>
- **Lawrence B. Solum, "Legal Personhood for Artificial Intelligences" (1992)**,
  70 *N.C. L. Rev.* 1231.
  <https://scholarship.law.unc.edu/nclr/vol70/iss4/4/>
- **Margaret Jane Radin, "Property and Personhood" (1982)** — nominated with an
  interest in updating it for the present.
  <https://cyber.harvard.edu/IPCoop/82radi.html>
- **Carol M. Rose, "Crystals and Mud in Property Law" (1988)**, 40 *Stan. L. Rev.*
  577 — offered as "not quite personhood, but related to the question of boundary
  conditions": property rules oscillate between hard-edged "crystal" rules and muddy
  standards. Speaks directly to the observation from the first session that identity
  often starts as a porous boundary and is later rendered crystal clear.
  <https://www.jstor.org/stable/1228813>
- **"What is a Tort?", *Harvard Law Review* vol. 139** — offered as relevant to how
  tort law reshaped corporate personhood.
  <https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-139/what-is-a-tort/>
- **Paul Schiff Berman, "Rats, Pigs, and Statues on Trial: The Creation of Cultural
  Narratives in the Prosecution of Animals and Inanimate Objects" (1994)**, 69
  *N.Y.U. L. Rev.* 288 — the trials of animals and objects held across Europe from the
  ninth to the nineteenth centuries, read as a way communities explained harm caused
  by nonhuman actors, and what that social function of trials suggests for the present.
  <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1430586>
- **Two working papers on AI, values, and liability** — the first reads Hume on the
  potential for values to emerge from repeated iteration against an objective or
  benchmark; the second considers existing liability regimes as applied to AI agents.
  <https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=7334643>
  <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7350103>
- **Old law school paper on tort liability and AI** — being updated by a group
  member, who may bring sources on historical models of tort liability as applied to
  animals, and on how corporations affected tort liability.
- **Papers on law and perspectivism** — recently completed by a group member and
  offered as non-Western and alternative-modern frameworks for personhood.
  *References to follow.*

## Philosophy and theory

- **David Hume, *Essays, Moral and Political* (1748)** — nominated as one of the
  earliest modern texts attempting to construct personhood.
  <https://davidhume.org/texts/empl1/nc>
- **"Boundary object"** — background for the question of whether personhood is one.
  <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_object>
- **"The Replacements: Capgras and the Anthropocene"**, Endnotes — on a world of
  adversarial doubles and surreptitious imitation.
  <https://endnotes.cc/p/the-replacements-capgras-and-the>
- **Jeffrey Jerome Cohen (ed.), *Monster Theory: Reading Culture*** (University of
  Minnesota Press, 1996) — offered alongside the above. Cohen's opening essay,
  "Monster Culture (Seven Theses)", makes the monster the harbinger of category
  crisis, which is the frame being borrowed here.
  <https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5749/j.ctttsq4d>
- **Elizabeth A. Povinelli, *Geontologies: A Requiem to Late Liberalism*** (Duke
  University Press, 2016) — raised in the first session for the anecdote of the rock
  formation "Two Women Sitting Down", destroyed by a mining company, and the legal
  system's inability to hold both frameworks at once: destruction of property versus
  murder of a being.
- **"A Good Death" essay**, Summer of Protocols — described as especially generative.
  <https://summerofprotocols.com/good-death-web>
- **G. W. F. Hegel, *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (1820)** — the source Radin's
  "Property and Personhood" (under law, above) is working from: Hegel treats property
  as the first external embodiment of the person's freedom.
- **Kwame Gyekye, *An Essay on African Philosophical Thought: The Akan Conceptual
  Scheme*** (Cambridge University Press, 1987; revised edition Temple University Press,
  1995) — includes a sustained account of the Akan concept of a person.
- **Giorgio Agamben, *The Use of Bodies*** (Stanford University Press, 2016; Italian
  2014), trans. Adam Kotsko — the concluding volume of the *Homo Sacer* series.
- **Reza Negarestani, *Intelligence and Spirit*** (Urbanomic/Sequence Press, 2018) — an
  account of general intelligence, human or artificial, built from Hegel, Sellars, and
  Brandom.
- **Joshua Bennett, *Being Property Once Myself: Blackness and the End of Man*** (Belknap
  Press of Harvard University Press, 2020) — reads the animals of African American
  literature as a critique of the category of "Man".

## Anthropology, history, and social theory

- **Marcel Mauss, "A Category of the Human Mind: The Notion of Person; the Notion of
  Self" (1938)** — the Huxley Memorial Lecture, tracing the category of the person from
  masks and roles, through the Roman *persona* as a legal status, to the modern self.
  English translation by W. D. Halls in Michael Carrithers, Steven Collins & Steven
  Lukes (eds.), *The Category of the Person: Anthropology, Philosophy, History*
  (Cambridge University Press, 1985).
- **Ian Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986)**, in Thomas C. Heller, Morton Sosna & David
  E. Wellbery (eds.), *Reconstructing Individualism* (Stanford University Press) — new
  ways of classifying people bring new kinds of people into being. Hacking returned to
  the argument in a 2006 *London Review of Books* essay of the same title.
  <https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v28/n16/ian-hacking/making-up-people>
- **Harvey Sacks, "On Doing 'Being Ordinary'"**, in J. Maxwell Atkinson & John Heritage
  (eds.), *Structures of Social Action* (Cambridge University Press, 1984) — being
  ordinary as work people actively do, not a default state.
- **Caroline Walker Bynum, *The Resurrection of the Body in Western Christianity,
  200–1336*** (Columbia University Press, 1995) — medieval debates over bodily
  resurrection, and what they show about identity understood as bound to the material
  body.
- **Hans Belting, *An Anthropology of Images: Picture, Medium, Body*** (Princeton
  University Press, 2011; German *Bild-Anthropologie*, 2001), trans. Thomas Dunlap —
  images, their media, and the body, including images made to stand in for the bodies of
  the dead.

## Life, sentience, and intelligence

- **Reed Bender, Karina Kofman, Blaise Agüera y Arcas & Michael Levin, "What Lives? A
  Meta-Analysis of Diverse Opinions on the Definition of Life" (2026)**, *Biological
  Theory* — uses LLMs to cluster definitions of life collected from experts across
  disciplines, and finds a continuous landscape of themes, suggesting that what has been
  treated as a binary taxonomic problem is better seen as differentiated perspectives in
  one conceptual space. Open access; preprint 2025.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/s13752-026-00544-9>
  <https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15849>
- **Gregory Chaitin, "Toward a Mathematical Definition of 'Life'" (1979)**, in R. D.
  Levine & M. Tribus (eds.), *The Maximum Entropy Formalism* (MIT Press) — borrows
  compression and randomness from algorithmic information theory to measure the
  "organized complexity" of simple structures, as groundwork for studying the origins of
  life mathematically.
  <https://www.organism.earth/library/document/mathematical-definition-of-life>
- **Jonathan Birch, *The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals,
  and AI*** (Oxford University Press, 2024) — how to act under uncertainty about which
  beings are sentient, across disorders of consciousness, fetuses, neural organoids,
  invertebrates, and AI. Open access.
  <https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-edge-of-sentience-9780192870421>
- **N. Katherine Hayles, *Unthought: The Power of the Cognitive Nonconscious*** (University
  of Chicago Press, 2017) — cognition without consciousness, in humans, other organisms,
  and technical systems.
- **Karawynn Long, "Language Is a Poor Heuristic for Intelligence" (2023)**, *Nine
  Lives* — treating fluency in language as a measure of intelligence has long harmed
  disabled people, nonspeaking autistic and deaf people especially, and LLMs now make
  that mistake everyone's problem.
  <https://ninelives.karawynnlong.com/p/language-is-a-poor-heuristic-for-intelligence>
- **Rua M. Williams, "All Robots Are Disabled"**, in *Social Robots in Social
  Institutions: Proceedings of Robophilosophy 2022* — how disability stigma shapes the
  way people relate to robots, and a proposal for "cripped" human–robot relations
  drawing on disabled cyborg scholarship.
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/FAIA220622>

## Collectives and complex systems

- **Cosma Rohilla Shalizi, "Scaling and Hierarchy in Urban Economies" (2011)** — a
  re-analysis of the claim by Bettencourt, West and collaborators that urban economic
  output scales super-linearly with population. The data cannot tell power laws from
  other forms; the apparent scaling is largely an artefact of using city-wide totals
  rather than per-capita rates; and what size effect remains is explained by
  high-value specialist industries concentrating in larger cities.
  <https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.4101>
- **Natalia Vélez, Charley M. Wu, Samuel J. Gershman & Eric Schulz, "The rise and fall of
  technological development in virtual communities" (2024)** — 2,700 communities built
  from scratch in the multiplayer game *One Hour One Life*. Communities survive by
  balancing diversification, which develops new technologies, against specialisation,
  which maintains existing ones; imbalances in specialisation are an early sign of
  population collapse.
  <https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tz4dn>

## Digital identity and proof of personhood

Nominated on the grounds that determining something *has* personhood is only half the
problem: it is also getting increasingly difficult to prove you are that thing in a
digital context.

- **"Proof of personhood: What it is and why it's needed"**, World (Worldcoin),
  21 February 2024 — the case for verifying humanness and uniqueness online as AI
  advances, and for World ID's iris-biometric protocol as the way to do it.
  <https://world.org/blog/world/proof-of-personhood-what-it-is-why-its-needed>
- **Puja Ohlhaver, Mikhail Nikulin & Paula Berman, "Compressed to 0: The Silent
  Strings of Proof of Personhood" (2024)** — the counterweight. Chronicles Idena's
  proof-of-personhood experiment from its 2019 launch to a crisis in May 2022: the
  protocol verified humans successfully, but hidden pools emerged in which
  "puppeteers" paid people \$2–\$14 every few weeks for their secret keys and ran
  their accounts. By May 2022, 23 entities — under 0.6% of the network's distinct
  entities — controlled some 40% of accounts and half the rewards. Filtering humans
  from bots turned out not to touch the harder problem of filtering humans acting
  like bots.
  <https://ssrn.com/abstract=4749892>
  (annotated copy: <https://ia601600.us.archive.org/35/items/elopio-papers/2024-compressed_to_0-annotated.pdf>)

## Fiction

- **Annalee Newitz, *The Terraformers* (2023)** — turns on this topic directly: its
  world rests on a historical event, the Great Bargain, during which humanity learned
  to endow other creatures (from robots to animals) with human-level intelligence, so
  the "who speaks for the trees" problem is partly dissolved by technology — the
  moose, the cat, the train can literally talk. Two reviews if the novel itself is too
  long for a session:
  <https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/a-different-bargain-with-nature-on-annalee-newitzs-the-terraformers/>
  <https://www.andrewliptak.com/annalee-newitz-the-terrraformers-sci-fi-book-review/>
- **Ray Nayler, *The Mountain in the Sea*** — a meditation on nonhuman
  consciousness.
- **Ray Nayler, *The Tusks of Extinction*** — novella, on related ground.
- **Stanisław Lem, *The Invincible*** — the necroevolved swarm: collective cognition
  and intelligence without apparent sentience, consciousness, or experience.
- **Peter Watts, *Blindsight*** — same problem from the other direction.
